The pricing page is not where you lose the deal

Six months of call recordings said the objection was price. The transcripts said something else entirely, and the fix turned out to be three sentences sitting above the fold.

RW By Rosa Whitfield · 14 January 2026 · 9 min read

We pulled six months of recordings because the team had agreed on a story. Deals were stalling at the pricing conversation, so the price was wrong, so the answer was a cheaper tier. That is a comfortable conclusion because it is cheap to act on and nobody has to admit the product was explained badly.

Then we read the transcripts instead of trusting the summaries. In thirty one of the thirty eight stalled deals, the buyer had asked some version of the same question in the first nine minutes. Not how much does it cost. It was closer to who else on my team needs to care about this.

That is not a pricing objection. That is a person trying to work out whether they can defend the purchase to somebody who was not on the call. They were not stalling because the number was too big. They were stalling because they could not repeat back what they would be buying.

The objection you hear is rarely the one being made

Buyers are polite. Price is the socially acceptable way to end a conversation, because it implies the thing is good and the timing is wrong. Nobody says I did not follow your explanation. So the real reason gets laundered into a budget problem on its way out of the room, and your CRM faithfully records the laundered version.

If a buyer cannot describe your product in one sentence to their boss, you do not have a pricing problem. You have a sentence problem.

From the Positioning Teardown worksheet

The test is uncomfortable and takes about four minutes. Go to the last five deals you lost. Find the moment the buyer restates what you do in their own words. If that restatement is vague, or if it never happens at all, the deal was gone long before anybody opened a pricing page.

What we actually changed

Nothing about the price. We rewrote four things, in this order, and shipped them over about nine days:

  1. The first line of the homepage, so it named the buyer and the moment rather than the category.
  2. A one paragraph block under it that answered, in plain words, what the thing replaces. Buyers reason by substitution, not by feature.
  3. The sales deck slide two, which had been the company timeline. It became the same sentence as the homepage, on purpose.
  4. A short section in the proposal template written for the person who was never on the call. That is the person who kills deals.

The part that surprised us

Close rate moved, but slowly, and honestly within the range where you cannot claim causation. The thing that moved sharply was cycle time. Deals that closed, closed about eleven days faster on average, because the internal selling that used to happen without us now had a script that survived being forwarded.

The second surprise was internal. Once one sentence was agreed, the arguments about tone and length mostly stopped. Most copy debates are positioning debates that nobody has named yet.

If you want to try this

Do not start with the homepage. Start with the recordings, because the sentence you need is almost always already in them, said by a customer, in worse grammar than you would have chosen. Take it anyway. The grammar is not the point.

Then go and read what your own pricing page is being asked to do. In most cases it is being asked to finish an explanation that should have ended four screens earlier. That is not a job a table of tiers can do.
